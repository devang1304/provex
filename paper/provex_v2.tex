\begin{abstract}
Provenance-based intrusion detectors built on temporal graph networks (TGNs), such as KAIROS, flag
system events whose type the model finds improbable given each entity's history. Analysts are not told
\emph{why} an event was improbable. We show that applying standard GNN explainers to such detectors is
subtly wrong: they search for evidence that the event looks \emph{normal}. They also ignore the TGN
memory, which carries most of the history.

PROVEX explains the anomaly score itself. It has three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an anomaly-preserving and a counterfactual objective over past events;
\item an exact, differentiable replay of the TGN memory update, which can delete any past event inside a
history window (validated against full re-execution);
\item a variational explainer with a temporal prior whose uncertainty we test for calibration.
\end{enumerate}
We also provide a faithful GraphMask for attention-based message passing.

We retrain KAIROS with \NumSeeds{} seeds on DARPA TC E3 CADETS. It detects \NumAttacksDetected{} of the
four attacks under its own alert rule, and we explain every alert it raises. We report:
\begin{itemize}
\item faithfulness against six baselines;
\item agreement with node-level ground truth;
\item stability and calibration;
\item cost.
\end{itemize}
We also report a property of the detector that explanation exposes: \FracPriorAlerts{} of alerted
events remain anomalous with \emph{no} history at all, so no history-based explanation can account
for them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Provenance-based intrusion detection systems (PIDS) represent system activity as a graph of processes,
files and sockets, and learn what normal activity looks like~\cite{kairos,unicorn,orthrus,bilot}.
KAIROS~\cite{kairos} is representative. A temporal graph network (TGN)~\cite{tgn} keeps a memory
vector per entity and predicts the \emph{type} of each new event from the memories and recent
neighbourhoods of its two endpoints. The event's anomaly score is the cross-entropy of its true type.
High-scoring events in 15-minute windows are linked into queues, and an alert is raised when a queue's
score exceeds a threshold.

An alert alone tells an analyst \emph{that} something is improbable, not \emph{why}. A natural answer
would identify which past events made the flagged event improbable. Post-hoc GNN explainers such as
GNNExplainer~\cite{gnnexplainer} and GraphMask~\cite{graphmask} appear to provide this. We argue they
do not, for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The objective is inverted.} These explainers find the subgraph that best supports the
  \emph{observed} label. For an anomaly detector that scores $-\log p(\text{observed})$, that is the
  evidence that the event is normal: the opposite of what explains the alert.
\item \textbf{They miss the memory.} TGN memory is a recurrent summary of everything an entity has done.
  Masking only the last-hop neighbourhood leaves the dominant channel unexplained.
\end{enumerate}

PROVEX addresses both. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Anomaly-faithful objectives} (\S\ref{sec:objectives}). A preserve objective finds a
  minimal set of past events that keeps the score above the alert threshold. A counterfactual objective
  finds a minimal set whose removal brings it below the threshold. Both are optimised in loss space with
  hard-concrete gates and verified by exact deletion.
\item \textbf{History gating} (\S\ref{sec:history}). We give a differentiable replay of the TGN memory
  update in evaluation mode, including its last-message aggregation and tie-breaking. A gate on a past
  event therefore means ``this event never happened''. With binary gates it is exact; we validate it
  against full re-execution of the detector.
\item \textbf{Calibrated uncertainty} (\S\ref{sec:variational}). A variational explainer with a temporal
  prior exports per-event inclusion probabilities, which we compare with exact posteriors obtained by
  enumeration.
\item \textbf{A faithful GraphMask for attention layers} (\S\ref{sec:graphmask}), which off-the-shelf
  implementations do not support.
\item \textbf{An evaluation} on all four CADETS~E3 attacks (\S\ref{sec:eval}). It covers:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item explanations driven by the detector's own alerts, on a KAIROS rebuilt from upstream with fixed seeds;
  \item node-level ground truth from ORTHRUS/PIDSMaker~\cite{orthrus,bilot};
  \item faithfulness, stability, calibration and cost;
  \item an \emph{alert taxonomy} that separates alerts explainable by recent history from alerts driven
    only by the event-type prior.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Code, patches to upstream KAIROS, trained models and all outputs are released.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Provenance-based detection.} StreamSpot~\cite{streamspot} and UNICORN~\cite{unicorn} summarise
graphs into sketches. HOLMES~\cite{holmes} correlates suspicious information flows with expert rules.
KAIROS~\cite{kairos} learns per-event anomaly scores with a TGN and reconstructs attack subgraphs by
community detection over anomalous windows. ORTHRUS~\cite{orthrus} targets \emph{quality of
attribution}: how much analyst effort an alert costs. Bilot et~al.~\cite{bilot} show that simpler
detectors match these systems on most DARPA datasets and release common ground truth, which we use.

\textbf{Explaining detections.} ProvExplainer~\cite{provexplainer} distils a GNN detector into
interpretable surrogates over security-aware subgraph features. ProvX~\cite{provx} searches for minimal
counterfactual perturbations that flip a GNN-based detection. Our counterfactual objective shares that
goal for a TGN-based, per-event detector. Its distinguishing elements are the treatment of memory and
the exactness check.

\textbf{GNN and temporal GNN explainers.} GNNExplainer~\cite{gnnexplainer} learns soft edge masks;
GraphMask~\cite{graphmask} learns amortised hard-concrete gates with learned baselines;
PGExplainer~\cite{pgexplainer} amortises mask prediction; CF-GNNExplainer~\cite{cfgnnexplainer} learns
minimal deletions that flip a prediction. For temporal graphs, T-GNNExplainer~\cite{tgnnexplainer}
searches subsets of past events with an explorer--navigator scheme, and TempME~\cite{tempme} explains
with temporal motifs under an information bottleneck. PROVEX differs in what it explains (the
cross-entropy anomaly score, not a predicted label) and in modelling the memory update exactly.
\section{Background: How KAIROS Scores Events}
\label{sec:background}
A day of audit data is a time-ordered stream of events $e_k=(u_k,v_k,y_k,t_k,\mathbf{r}_k)$. Here
$u_k,v_k$ are entities, $y_k$ is one of seven event types, and $\mathbf{r}_k$ is a raw message of hashed
endpoint features plus a one-hot type. KAIROS processes the stream in batches of $B{=}1024$. For a batch,
a last-neighbour loader gives, for every endpoint, its $K$ most recent interactions as in-edges. A
two-layer TransformerConv embeds the endpoints. Its inputs are memory states $\mathbf{s}$, and its edge
features are a cosine time encoding of $t_{\text{last}}(\text{src})-t$ concatenated with the raw
message. An MLP then predicts the type distribution $p(y\mid e)$. The anomaly score is
$L(e)=-\log p(y_e\mid e)$. Only after scoring does the memory update. Each node aggregates the
\emph{latest} message of the batch, $[\mathbf{s}_u,\mathbf{s}_c,\mathbf{r},\phi(t-t_{\text{last}})]$, and
applies a GRU. Memory and loader reset at the start of each day.

Windows close at batch boundaries once 15~minutes have elapsed. Events with $L\ge\tau_w$, where
$\tau_w=\mu_w+1.5\sigma_w$ over the window, are anomalous. Windows sharing rare anomalous entities are
linked into queues, and a queue whose score exceeds $\beta$ raises an alert~\cite{kairos}.

\section{What Should an Explanation of an Alert Explain?}
\label{sec:objectives}
Let $\mathcal{I}(e)$ be the \emph{items} that can influence $L(e)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the neighbour edges of the event's two-hop computation graph;
\item the past events inside a history window that updated the memory of the endpoints, or of entities
  that later fed them (\S\ref{sec:history}).
\end{itemize}
An explanation assigns importance to items. We write $L(\mathbf{g})$ for the score when item $i$ is
kept with gate $g_i\in[0,1]$, so that $L(\mathbf{1})=L(e)$.

\textbf{The standard objective is inverted.} GNNExplainer-style methods in ``phenomenon'' mode minimise
$\mathrm{CE}(f(G\odot\mathbf m), y_e)$. For a classifier, that finds evidence \emph{for} the predicted
label. For an anomaly score $L=-\log p(y_e)$, minimising the same quantity finds the items whose
presence makes the observed type \emph{likely}: the evidence that the event is normal. Fidelity metrics
in probability space, such as $p_{\text{full}}-p_{\text{drop}}$, are also degenerate for anomalies,
because $p_{\text{full}}=e^{-L}$ is tiny. For example, $L\ge 5$ already gives $p\le 0.007$.

\textbf{Anomaly-faithful objectives.} We define two objectives with margin $\delta$ and temperature $T$:
\begin{align}
\text{preserve:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf g}\ \textstyle\sum_i g_i \ \ \text{s.t.}\ L(\mathbf g)\ge\min(L(\mathbf 1),\tau+\delta),\\
\text{counterfactual:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf g}\ \textstyle\sum_i (1-g_i) \ \ \text{s.t.}\ L(\mathbf g)\le\tau-\delta.
\end{align}
Preserve gives a minimal set of past events sufficient to sustain the alert. Counterfactual gives a
minimal set whose absence would have prevented it. We relax the constraint violation with
$T\,\mathrm{softplus}(\cdot/T)$. Softplus keeps gradients alive where a hinge would be flat. Working in
loss space avoids $-\log(1-p)$, whose gradient vanishes for $p\approx 10^{-11}$.

\textbf{Alert taxonomy.} Not every alert is explained by recent history. We compute three scores:
\begin{itemize}
\item $L_\varnothing$: all items removed;
\item $L_{\text{prior}}$: all memories zeroed and all edges removed, i.e.\ the type prior alone;
\item the full score.
\end{itemize}
An alert is \emph{window-explainable} if $L_\varnothing<\tau$: removing the whole window flips it, so a
counterfactual exists inside the window. Otherwise it is \emph{prior-driven} if $L_{\text{prior}}\ge\tau$
and \emph{long-memory-driven} if not. For these two classes the empty set already sustains the alert, so
the preserve explanation is empty. Removing the whole window does not flip them, but $L$ is not monotone
in the removed set, so a smaller removal still might. We therefore run the counterfactual search on every
alert and report how often it finds one outside the window-explainable class (\CFFoundNonWindow{}).

\section{Exact, Differentiable History Gating}
\label{sec:history}
\textbf{Replay that matches the detector.} PROVEX re-executes KAIROS exactly. It uses the same batch
partition, per-day resets, window boundaries and per-event losses, verified bit-for-bit against the
detector's own window files. For each event to explain, it captures two things. The first is the
\emph{exact receptive field}: the in-edges of $T=\{u,v\}\cup N_1$, where $N_1$ are the in-neighbours of
$u,v$; this is exactly what a two-layer convolution reads. The second is the memory-update history
inside a window of $H$ batches. Every reported item is keyed by its absolute event index and global
node identifiers.

\textbf{Influence closure.} The memory of $u$ at the event depends on the memories of the counterparts
$c$ that sent it messages earlier. We therefore replay the memory of the closure $S$, computed by
walking batches backwards: if $i\in S$ and $(i,c)$ occurs in batch $b$, then $c$ joins $S$ for all
earlier batches. $S$ is capped at 64 nodes, and a truncated closure is flagged.

\textbf{Gated last-message aggregation.} For a node $i\in S$ in batch $b$, let its candidate messages
be ordered as PyG resolves ties: by time descending, then by store order. With gates $g_k$ we set
\[
w_k=g_k\prod_{j<k}(1-g_j),\qquad P=1-\prod_{k\in\text{current}}(1-g_k),
\]
\[
\mathbf s_i'=P\,\frac{\sum_k w_k\,\mathrm{GRU}(\mathbf m_k,\mathbf s_i)}{\sum_k w_k}+(1-P)\,\mathbf s_i .
\]
With binary gates this is exactly the detector's update. Deleting the latest message promotes the
next-latest. Deleting all of a node's current events leaves it out of the batch, so it is not updated.
Last-update times follow the hard gates without gradient: KAIROS encodes time as $\cos(\mathbf w\,\Delta t)$
with $\Delta t\sim10^{12}$\,ns, so gradients through a relaxed timestamp are noise. We also subtract
timestamps in 64-bit integers before converting to floating point. Doing it the other way round changes
logits by more than one nat on real data.

\textbf{Exactness.} With a complete closure, deleting any event in the window reproduces a full
re-execution of the detector with that event removed. We validate this in two places:
\begin{itemize}
\item a synthetic float64 suite, where all \NumExactDeletionsTest{} checked deletions are identical;
\item real alerts (\S\ref{sec:eval}, E7).
\end{itemize}
After a deletion the neighbour loader is not back-filled with older neighbours; this is a stated
limitation.

\textbf{True edge deletion.} PyG's explanation hook scales messages \emph{after} the attention softmax,
so a zero mask is not deletion. We rescale each surviving message by the attention mass that remains at
its target, and zero targets with no surviving in-edges. With binary masks this equals running the
layer on the pruned graph.

\section{Explainers}
\label{sec:variational}
\label{sec:graphmask}
\textbf{Joint explainer.} The joint explainer uses hard-concrete gates~\cite{louizos} over all items. It
minimises expected $L_0$ under the preserve or counterfactual constraint, with a persistent Lagrange
multiplier. The result is checked by exact hard deletion. If it is invalid, a greedy repair adds or
removes items in score order, and we report the repair.

\textbf{Variational temporal explainer.} We place a Gaussian posterior over the gate logits,
$\zeta_k\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$, with $m_k=\mathrm{clip}(1.2\,\sigma(\zeta_k)-0.1)$. The
\emph{temporal prior} is $\mathcal N(a-\kappa\log(1+\Delta t_k/60\text{s}),1)$, which makes older events
a priori less likely to be needed. The likelihood $\sigma((L(\mathbf m)-\tau)/T)$ is the probability
that the alert holds. We maximise the ELBO and export, per item, the posterior inclusion probability
$\pi_k=\Phi(\mu_k/\sigma_k)$, $\mathbb E[m_k]$ and $\mathrm{Std}[m_k]$. For calibration, we enumerate all
$2^n$ binary masks over the $n$ most uncertain items ($n\le 12$) to obtain exact posterior marginals,
and report expected calibration error.

\textbf{GraphMask for TransformerConv.} Off-the-shelf GraphMask hooks assume a layer's propagate receives
node features. TransformerConv instead passes query, key and value, so those hooks fail on it. Our
version follows GraphMask~\cite{graphmask}:
\begin{itemize}
\item amortised per-layer gate networks read $(\mathbf q_i,\mathbf k_j{+}\mathbf e,\mathbf v_j{+}\mathbf e)$
  and the edge's role for the explained event (whether its target or source is an endpoint). Without the
  role, an amortised gate cannot tell which edges reach a link prediction (in the last layer only edges
  into the two endpoints do), and training degenerates to one switch per layer;
\item a gated-off edge has its attention score and value replaced by learned baselines, with the softmax
  recomputed;
\item training minimises $L_0$ subject to $|L_{\text{masked}}-L|/\max(L,1)\le 5\%$ on anomalous events
  sampled across the validation day;
\item training starts on the feasible side, with a large initial Lagrange multiplier and a slow dual step,
  and log-$\alpha$ is bounded by a leaky clamp. With the usual initialisation every gate closes in the first
  epoch and never reopens, since a fully closed hard-concrete gate receives no gradient. As in GraphMask,
  the constraint holds on average over events. Gates can still collapse later in training, so we keep the
  sparsest epoch whose deviation on held-out validation events meets the constraint.
\end{itemize}
It answers which neighbour edges the model \emph{needs}, and does not cover memory. On held-out
validation events, replacing every neighbour edge with the learned baselines changes $L$ by
\GMAllClosedDev{} on average and already meets the allowance on \GMAllClosedValid{} of them. Trained on
\GMTrainEvents{} events, the selected gates keep \GMOpenFrac{} of the edges open and meet the allowance on
\GMValidFrac{} of held-out events.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against the following, all on the same items:
\begin{itemize}
\item random, recency, attention rollout, gradient, integrated gradients and leave-one-out occlusion;
\item PyG GNNExplainer with our preserve objective (neighbour edges only);
\item a temporal-GNN explainer in the style of T-GNNExplainer~\cite{tgnnexplainer}: Monte-Carlo tree
  search over subsets of at most 10 of the same items, scored by the anomaly with only the subset kept.
  We replace its learned navigator with a gradient prior, so this baseline needs no training;
\item the v1 configuration (GNNExplainer minimising cross-entropy of the true type), as a documented
  counter-example.
\end{itemize}
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\textbf{Data and detector.} We use DARPA TC Engagement~3 CADETS~\cite{darpa}: day graphs for 2018-04-02
to 04-13, and four attacks (04-06, 04-11, 04-12 and 04-13). Node-level ground truth comes from
ORTHRUS/PIDSMaker~\cite{orthrus,bilot} and is mapped to KAIROS nodes by name, which is how KAIROS keys
them.

We rebuild KAIROS from upstream at a pinned commit, with documented patches: seeds, all test days, all
four attacks, and PyG~2.7 compatibility. We use the upstream hyper-parameters and train on 04-02 to
04-04 with \NumSeeds{} seeds. Days 04-03 to 04-05 initialise the rare-node scores of the alert queues,
04-05 is the validation day, and 04-06 to 04-13 are test days. Under its own alert rule, KAIROS detects
\NumAttacksDetected{} of the four attacks (\AttacksDetected{}; Table~\ref{tab:detection}). We explain
every alert it raises, true and false positives alike; the missed attacks have no alert to explain.

\textbf{What is explained.} We explain the events KAIROS alerts on. These are the anomalous events
($L\ge\tau_w$) in every window of every alerted queue. For each window we take the top \TopN{} events by
score plus a uniform sample of \SampleN{} more, covering true and false positives alike.

\textbf{Experiments.}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scope of history.} History gating covers a window of $H$ batches and a capped influence
  closure. Truncated closures are flagged, and E7 measures their error. Deleted events do not back-fill
  the neighbour loader.
\item \textbf{One detector, one host.} We study KAIROS on CADETS~E3 only. DARPA~E3 data is small and
  well studied~\cite{bilot}, and conclusions may not transfer to other datasets or detectors.
\item \textbf{Names, not UUIDs.} Ground truth is mapped by entity name. KAIROS merges entities with the
  same name, such as all \texttt{nginx} processes, so entity-level ground truth is coarser than
  UUID-level.
\item \textbf{No user study.} We measure faithfulness and agreement with ground truth, not analyst
  benefit.
\item \textbf{Nondeterminism.} GPU training is seeded but not bit-reproducible, because scatter kernels
  are nondeterministic. We therefore replay and explain on CPU and report variation across seeds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Explaining a temporal-graph anomaly detector requires explaining the \emph{anomaly score} and the
\emph{memory}, not a label and a neighbourhood. PROVEX provides anomaly-faithful objectives, exact
history gating, and calibrated uncertainty, and evaluates them on every alert of a rebuilt KAIROS. The
same analysis shows how many alerts no history can explain.
